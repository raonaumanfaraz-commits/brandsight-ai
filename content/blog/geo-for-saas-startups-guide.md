SaaS buyers have changed how they shop. Before they open Google, many ask ChatGPT or Gemini a question like "What is the best invoicing tool for a small agency?" The answer they get shapes the shortlist, and most startups are not in it yet. Generative engine optimization, or GEO, is the work of getting your product into those answers. For a SaaS startup with a small team, it is one of the cheapest growth channels available, because it runs on content and clarity rather than ad spend.

## Start with the questions buyers actually ask

GEO begins with prompts, not keywords. Write down the 20 questions your ideal customer asks an AI assistant when they are looking for a product like yours. Include direct questions ("What is the best CRM for freelancers?"), comparison questions ("Tool A vs Tool B"), and problem questions ("How do I automate client onboarding?"). Use the exact wording a customer would use, because AI models match answers to natural questions, not to search terms.

Then check where you stand. Ask those questions to ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek yourself, and note whether your product appears, whether it is recommended, and which sources get cited. That baseline is your starting point, and you should repeat the check weekly. If you are not mentioned at all, the fix is usually content. If you are mentioned but never recommended, the fix is usually proof: clearer positioning, real customer stories, and pages that explain who your product is for.

## Build pages that AI can quote

AI answers are built from sources the model can read and trust. Your website needs pages that answer buyer questions directly, in plain language, with a clear structure. A pricing page that states the price, a comparison page that honestly compares you with alternatives, and a use-case page for each customer type all help. Put the key facts near the top of the page, use short paragraphs and simple headings, and say what your product does in one sentence.

Third-party mentions matter too. AI models weigh independent sources heavily, so a listing on a software directory, a mention in a review roundup, or a quote in an industry article can carry more weight than your own homepage. Pick five directories and publications your buyers trust, and make sure your product is listed with an accurate, up-to-date description.

## Track mentions weekly, not monthly

AI answers change fast. A model update, a new competitor page, or a fresh review can move your product in or out of an answer within days. Check your prompt set every week and keep a simple log: mentioned or not, recommended or not, cited sources. When a prompt flips against you, find the new source the model is citing and respond to it, either by improving your own page or by earning a mention in that source.

For a startup, the routine is small: 20 prompts, 30 minutes a week, one person. The payoff is that you catch problems while they are still cheap to fix, and you learn which content actually moves the needle.

## What to do this week

Pick your 20 prompts, run them through the three main AI assistants, and write down the results. Then build or fix one page that answers the most important question your product was missing from. Repeat weekly, and within a month you will have a clear picture of where AI search puts you and what to fix next.
