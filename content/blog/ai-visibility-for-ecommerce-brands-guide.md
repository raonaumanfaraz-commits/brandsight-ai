Shoppers used to compare products by scrolling search results. Now many of them ask an AI assistant a question like "best running shoes under $100" and buy whatever it recommends. If your product is not named in that answer, it is not in the running at all. For e-commerce brands, AI visibility is no longer a branding metric. It is a sales channel.

## Track product prompts, not just brand prompts

Most brands start by tracking their own name. That is not enough for a store. Shoppers rarely ask for a brand by name before they have decided. They ask for a category, a use case, or a budget: "best waterproof hiking boots", "quiet blender for small kitchens", "gifts for a new dad under $50".

Build your prompt list around the buying questions your customers actually ask. Include category prompts, comparison prompts ("X vs Y"), budget prompts, and problem prompts ("how to stop shoes slipping on wet floors"). Then add brand prompts and competitor prompts so you can see where you stand against the names shoppers hear about most.

## Watch shopping answers specifically

AI shopping answers behave differently from general answers. They name specific products, compare prices and features, and often link to retailers. A mention of your brand in a general answer is nice, but a recommendation of your exact product in a shopping answer is what moves revenue.

When you review your tracking results, separate the two. Note which products get recommended, which prompts trigger them, and which competitors appear alongside them. If a competitor's product is recommended for a prompt where yours should win, that prompt is your next content job.

## Fix the pages AI cites

AI answers cite pages. When your product is missing from an answer, the cause is often that the page AI would need to cite does not exist or does not answer the question clearly.

Check which pages AI cites for your category prompts. Then make sure each of your key products has a page that states, in plain language, who it is for, what problem it solves, how it compares with the obvious alternatives, and what it costs. Product descriptions written only for human shoppers often skip the comparison and use-case detail that AI answers rely on. Add it.

Reviews help too. AI systems weigh what other people say about a product, so genuine customer reviews on your own pages and on independent review sites strengthen the case for recommending you.

## Measure what matters to a store

Vanity metrics will not tell you whether AI visibility is paying off. Track four things. First, mention rate: how often your brand or products appear in answers for your tracked prompts. Second, recommendation rate: how often you are named as the pick, not just mentioned in passing. Third, share of voice against your top three competitors for the same prompts. Fourth, the trend over time, because a single week of answers tells you little.

Check weekly at minimum. Daily checks are better during a launch or a sale, when AI answers can shift as new pages and reviews appear.

## Start small and expand

You do not need to track hundreds of prompts on day one. Start with ten to twenty buying questions that cover your best-selling categories, plus your brand name and your two closest competitors. Run them for a month and see which prompts you win, which you lose, and which return no brand at all.

Then expand the list in the direction of the gaps. If you win category prompts but lose comparison prompts, add more comparison content. If you are invisible for budget prompts, check whether your prices are stated clearly on the pages AI can read.

AI visibility for e-commerce is a routine, not a campaign. The stores that win are the ones that check every week, fix the pages AI cites, and keep their product information clear enough for an assistant to recommend with confidence.
