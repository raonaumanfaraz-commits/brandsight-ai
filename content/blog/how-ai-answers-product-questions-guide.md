A buyer used to type a question into Google and scan ten blue links. Now they ask ChatGPT or Gemini a full question, like "which CRM is best for a five-person sales team," and get one written answer. Your brand either appears in that answer, with a reason, or it does not appear at all. There is no page two.

Understanding how that answer is built is the first step to getting mentioned in it. The process is different from search ranking in almost every way that matters.

## The answer is assembled, not retrieved

An AI assistant does not fetch a page and show it to you. It reads your question, decides what kind of answer you need, and then assembles one from what it knows. That knowledge comes from three places: what the model learned during training, what it finds through live web search, and what it can quote from sources it trusts.

For a product question, the model first works out the intent. "Best CRM for a small team" is a comparison question, so it looks for products that fit the constraints, weighs them against each other, and writes a shortlist with reasons. "Does Brand X integrate with Slack" is a fact question, so it looks for one specific answer and cites the source. The shape of your question decides the shape of the answer, and which brands get a chance to appear.

## Why some brands get named and others do not

The model names brands it has seen mentioned often, in clear and consistent contexts. If dozens of articles, reviews, comparisons and forum threads describe your product the same way, the model has a strong picture of what it is and who it is for. If your product is described differently everywhere, or barely described at all, the model has nothing solid to repeat, so it picks a competitor it can describe confidently.

Citations matter more than mentions. When an AI assistant uses live search, it quotes the sources it actually opened. Pages that answer the question directly, in plain language, with facts and numbers, are the ones that get cited. A page that buries the answer under marketing copy, or that never states the price, the limits, or who the product is for, gives the model nothing to quote, so it quotes someone else's page instead.

Recency is the third filter. Training data has a cut-off date, so a brand that launched or changed after that date only shows up if live search finds it. A product page updated this year, a fresh comparison, or a recent review all help the model treat your brand as current rather than historical.

## What to do about it

Start by asking the assistants your buyers use the exact questions your buyers ask. Ask about your category, your competitors, and your product by name, and write down which brands appear, in what order, and with what reasons.

Then check the sources. When an answer cites a page, open it and ask whether your product could have been cited there instead. Usually the cited page answers the question better than yours does: it states the price, names the limits, and compares options in one place. Fix your own pages to answer the same questions that directly.

Finally, earn mentions where the model learns. Reviews, listicles, comparisons, community answers and roundups are the raw material for AI answers. A brand that appears in independent roundups with consistent facts gets named. A brand that only appears on its own website does not.

## The habit that keeps you visible

AI answers change as models retrain and as new pages get indexed, so a one-time check goes stale fast. A weekly or daily check of your core questions, with the same prompts each time, shows whether your visibility is rising, flat, or slipping, and whether a competitor just took your place in the shortlist.
