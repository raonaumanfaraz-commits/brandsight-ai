When an AI engine answers a buyer question, it does more than name brands. It calls one tool "the affordable option", another "enterprise-grade", and a third "limited". That tone is your AI brand sentiment, and it shapes what buyers believe before they visit your site.

There is no brand dashboard inside ChatGPT or Gemini, so measuring sentiment takes a small routine you can run in about an hour a week.

## Start with a fixed prompt list

Sentiment is only measurable if you ask the same questions every time. Pick 10 to 15 prompts your buyers actually ask, such as "best CRM for a small agency" or "alternatives to [your product]", mixing category, comparison, and direct questions about your brand.

Write the prompts down and do not change them for at least a month, or you will be measuring your prompts, not your brand.

## Ask in a clean session

Run the list once a week in a signed-out browser window or a fresh chat, so your own history does not steer the answers. Start with ChatGPT and Google AI Overviews, then Perplexity or Gemini if you have time.

Copy each answer into a sheet with the date, engine, prompt, and whether your brand was mentioned. A mention rate of zero is itself a finding, separate from sentiment.

## Score the tone, not just the mention

For every answer that mentions your brand, score the tone as positive, neutral, negative, or mixed.

- **Positive:** the answer recommends your brand or praises a strength.
- **Neutral:** your brand is listed with facts only, no praise and no criticism.
- **Negative:** the answer warns about a weakness or a bad experience.
- **Mixed:** the answer praises one thing and criticises another.

Two people scoring the same answers will disagree sometimes, so have the same person score every week, with a one-line reason next to each score.

## Watch for the words that move buyers

Beyond the score, note the exact words used about your brand: "affordable", "easy to set up", "limited integrations", "best for small teams". A single negative phrase repeated across engines matters more than a low score on one prompt.

Also note what your competitors are called. If a rival is consistently "the market leader" while you are "a cheaper alternative", that framing is a positioning problem from the sources the AI cites. Check the citations: they usually point to review sites or comparison pages.

## Track the trend, not the week

One week of scores is noise. Four weeks is a signal. Chart your share of positive, neutral, and negative mentions per engine, and look for movement after new content, a review, or a fixed weakness. Expect slow change: AI answers reflect the web, and the web changes gradually.

When negative sentiment persists, trace it to a source. If the AI cites an old review about a feature you have since fixed, ask the reviewer for an update or publish a page about the fix. If it cites a competitor's comparison page, publish your own honest comparison and earn links to it. Fixing sentiment means fixing the sources the AI reads.

## Turn sentiment into a weekly habit

Set aside the same hour every week. Run the list, score the answers, note the new phrases, and write three lines: what improved, what got worse, and what you will fix this week. Over a quarter, that habit gives you a record of how AI engines talk about your brand, and proof your work is changing it.
