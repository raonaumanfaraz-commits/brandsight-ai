When a customer asks ChatGPT for the best tool in your category, the answer names a handful of brands and leaves the rest out. If your competitors are in that answer and you are not, you are losing buyers before they ever reach a search results page. Competitor presence tracking means watching how often rival brands appear in AI answers, where they appear, and which sources the models cite for them. Done weekly, it turns guesswork about your market position into a number you can act on.

## Why track competitors in AI search

Traditional rank tracking tells you who wins on Google. AI answers work differently: a model picks a few brands, writes a short summary, and cites two or three sources. Being mentioned at all is the first battle, and being mentioned first or with a recommendation is the second. Tracking competitors shows you the gap in concrete terms. If a rival is mentioned in eight out of ten answers for your main buying prompt and you appear in two, you know the size of the problem and which prompts to work on first.

## What to track for each competitor

Start with three numbers per competitor. First, mention rate: in how many of your tracked prompts does the rival's name appear. Second, position: does the rival appear first, in the middle, or as an afterthought. Third, citations: which pages does the model link to when it mentions them. Citations are the most useful of the three because they point at the content that earned the mention. If a competitor is cited from a comparison article, a review site, or their own pricing page, that tells you where to compete. Add sentiment as a fourth signal: a mention that comes with a warning about pricing or support is not the same as a glowing recommendation.

## Setting up your prompt list

Pick ten to twenty prompts that real buyers actually ask. Mix three types: category questions ("best CRM for small teams"), comparison questions ("Tool A vs Tool B"), and problem questions ("how do I automate invoice reminders"). Run the same prompt list for your brand and for each competitor you want to watch. Keep the list fixed for at least a month so week-to-week changes mean something. Three to five competitors is enough for most small teams.

## Reading the results each week

Look at the trend, not a single day. AI answers vary from run to run, so one week of data is noise and four weeks is a signal. Each week, note three things: any competitor whose mention rate moved by more than ten points, any new source that started getting cited for a rival, and any prompt where your brand dropped out entirely. A sudden jump usually traces back to a specific event: a rival published a guide, earned a review, or got listed in a roundup.

## Turning findings into action

Tracking only pays off when it changes what you publish. If a competitor wins a prompt because of a detailed comparison page, write a better one. If they win because a review site ranks them first, pitch that site or improve the listing you already have. If the model cites your competitor's documentation while ignoring yours, check whether your own docs are crawlable and clearly written for AI readers. Share the weekly numbers with your team in one short update: who moved, why, and the one action for the week. Over a quarter, that routine compounds into real visibility gains.
