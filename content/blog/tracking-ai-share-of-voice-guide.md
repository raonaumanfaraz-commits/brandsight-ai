When someone asks ChatGPT for the best invoicing tool or the best dentist nearby, the assistant names two or three brands and moves on. The brands it names get the click and the sale. AI share of voice measures how often your brand is one of those names, compared with your competitors. Here is how to track it.

## What AI share of voice actually means

In classic SEO, share of voice is your share of impressions for a set of keywords. In AI search, it is your share of brand mentions across a fixed set of prompts. If you run 50 prompts about your market and your brand is mentioned in 20 answers while your main competitor appears in 30, your share is lower, and you can see exactly which questions you are losing.

Three numbers matter. Mention rate is how often you appear at all. Position is whether you are named first or as an afterthought. Citations are whether the assistant links to your own pages. A brand mentioned often but never cited is borrowing authority; a brand that is cited is building it.

## Pick the prompts that matter

Start with the questions your buyers actually ask. Write down 20 to 50 prompts in plain language, like "best CRM for a small agency" or "how to automate invoice reminders." Mix direct comparisons, "best of" lists, and problem questions where no brand is named at all. The problem questions are the most revealing, because they show which brands the assistant reaches for unprompted.

Keep the prompt list fixed for at least a month, or you cannot tell whether a change came from your work or from the new questions.

## Run the same prompts on the same engines

AI answers change between engines and between runs, so consistency is the whole method. Run the same prompt set on the same engines, ChatGPT and Gemini at minimum, on the same schedule. Daily checks catch sudden drops; weekly checks are enough for most small brands. Record each run the same way: brands mentioned, in what order, and pages cited.

A ChatGPT result from Monday and a Perplexity result from Friday is not a trend. Same prompts, same engines, same cadence, or the numbers mean nothing.

## Benchmark against competitors, not against perfection

A 100 percent mention rate is not the goal, and it is not realistic. The useful number is your share next to the three or four competitors your buyers actually compare you with, tracked on the same prompts in the same runs. If a competitor jumps from 30 to 50 percent mentions in a month, look at what changed on their side: new pages, new reviews, new coverage. That is your next content brief, written by the data.

## Act on what the numbers say

Share of voice is only useful if it changes what you publish. When a prompt never mentions you, ask why: is there no page on your site that answers it directly, or is the page thin and missing the facts the assistant quotes? Fix the page, put the answer in the first paragraph, and add the details assistants cite, like prices, comparisons, and steps.

When you are mentioned but never cited, the assistant trusts someone else's page more than yours. That usually means your page lacks original detail: real examples, current numbers, or clear headings that match the question. Add the detail only you can provide, then check the same prompt again in two weeks.

## Mistakes that make the numbers lie

The most common mistake is tracking too few prompts: five prompts produce noise, so aim for at least 20. The second is checking once and stopping; one run is a snapshot. The third is chasing every dip: a single bad week is usually noise, while a four-week slide on the same prompts is a signal.

Start simple: a fixed prompt list, the same engines, the same weekly rhythm. Within a month you will know which questions you win and lose, and which pages to fix first.
